# Lease Option Wholesaling — Deal Structure Research

## How Lease Option Wholesaling Works

A wholesale lease option (also called a **cooperative assignment**) combines two strategies: lease options and wholesaling. The investor locks up a property under a lease option agreement with the seller, then assigns that option to a tenant-buyer for an assignment fee. The investor never occupies or manages the property.

### End-to-End Flow

1. **Find motivated seller** willing to do a lease option
2. **Negotiate and sign** an Option to Purchase agreement with the seller
3. **Find a qualified tenant-buyer** whose criteria match the deal
4. **Assign the option** — tenant-buyer signs an Assignment of Lease Option, pays the assignment fee
5. **Collect profit** — investor keeps the assignment fee, exits the deal
6. **Seller and tenant-buyer** enter into a direct lease with option to purchase

The investor's exposure is limited to the time between locking the deal and finding a buyer. No financing needed.

## Seller Qualification Criteria

### Good Candidates (Sellers with "Good Debt")

Sellers NOT in financial desperation but with a life change motivating them to sell. Their problem is situational, not financial crisis.

| Criterion | Threshold | Notes |
|-----------|-----------|-------|
| **Equity position** | ≥ 10% equity | More equity = more room to negotiate price and terms. High equity sellers are ideal — can sell below market and still profit |
| **Motivation type** | Situational, not desperate | Job relocation, marriage (two homes), building new home, burned-out landlord, inherited property, divorce (amicable), military PCS |
| **Payment status** | Current on mortgage | Never more than 1 month behind. Sellers 2+ months behind are NOT lease option candidates |
| **Property condition** | Habitable, move-in ready | Minor cosmetic issues OK. Major structural/systems issues = disqualify |
| **Rental market viability** | Area supports rental demand | Property must be rentable at a price that covers or exceeds seller's mortgage payment |
| **Timeline flexibility** | Willing to wait 12-36 months | Seller must not need immediate cash-out. Typical lease option period is 18-24 months |
| **Openness to terms** | Will discuss creative structure | Seller willing to separate price from timing — "I'll give you your price if you give me my terms" |
| **Existing loan** | No prepayment penalty, no balloon | Loan must be serviceable during the option period |

### Motivation Signals (Positive Indicators)

- Relocating for work (especially corporate relocation with timeline pressure)
- Two homeowners getting married (need to sell one)
- Building a new home (need old one handled while new one completes)
- Burned-out landlord (wants out of property management)
- Inherited property (emotional distance, wants it off their hands)
- Listed for 90+ days with no offers (open to alternatives)
- FSBO with no traction
- Absentee owner with management problems

## Seller Disqualifiers

Any ONE of these = reject as lease option candidate:

1. **Behind on mortgage 2+ months** — financially distressed sellers are not good lease option candidates. Use subject-to or short sale instead.
2. **Needs immediate cash-out** — lease options require 12-36 months. If seller needs money now, this structure doesn't fit.
3. **Negative equity (underwater)** — owes more than the property is worth. No room for a deal.
4. **Property in severe disrepair** — uninhabitable, major foundation/roof/systems issues. Tenant-buyers expect move-in ready.
5. **HOA restrictions on leasing** — some HOAs prohibit or restrict lease-to-own arrangements. Verify before contracting.
6. **Seller unwilling to discuss terms** — only wants traditional sale at full price with no flexibility. Wrong strategy fit.
7. **Property in flood zone / uninsurable** — creates liability and financing problems for eventual tenant-buyer purchase.
8. **Pending litigation on property** — title issues, liens, lawsuits. Clean title required.
9. **Seller has unrealistic price expectations** — wants 20%+ above market and won't budge. Math doesn't work for tenant-buyer.
10. **Area has no rental demand** — rural, declining market, high vacancy. Can't attract tenant-buyers.

## Tenant-Buyer Qualification Criteria

The tenant-buyer is almost mortgage-ready but has one or two temporary obstacles. They need 12-36 months to become fully qualified.

| Criterion | Threshold | Notes |
|-----------|-----------|-------|
| **Credit score** | ≥ 530 current, with clear path to 620+ | FHA requires 580 for 3.5% down, conventional requires 620+. Tenant-buyer should be within 50 points of their target loan threshold |
| **Income stability** | ≥ 3x monthly rent in gross income | W-2 preferred. 1099/self-employed OK if 2+ year history. Must be documentable for eventual mortgage |
| **Debt-to-income ratio** | ≤ 45% (ideally ≤ 36%) | Fannie Mae standard: 36% maximum. Up to 45% allowed with higher credit + reserves |
| **Option fee capacity** | Can pay 3-5% of purchase price upfront | This is the non-refundable option consideration. Demonstrates financial commitment and skin in the game |
| **Employment history** | 2+ years same employer or field | Lenders require 2-year employment history. Recent job changers or newly self-employed need time |
| **Bankruptcy status** | No active bankruptcy. If discharged: 2+ years for FHA, 4+ for conventional | Active bankruptcy = automatic disqualify |
| **Reason for not qualifying now** | Specific, fixable issue | "My credit is 590 and I need 620" = good. "I have no income" = bad |
| **Timeline to mortgage-ready** | 12-36 months (realistic assessment) | Must have a plausible path. Mortgage broker pre-qualification recommended |

### Screening Approach

Best practice: have a **mortgage broker pre-qualify** the tenant-buyer. The broker assesses:
- Current credit score and what needs to change
- Income documentation readiness
- Down payment savings plan (option fee counts toward this)
- Realistic timeline to loan approval

This is more reliable than self-screening because the broker uses actual lending criteria.

## Tenant-Buyer Disqualifiers

1. **Active bankruptcy** — cannot enter into purchase agreements during active bankruptcy
2. **No verifiable income** — no W-2, no 1099, no bank statements showing deposits. Cannot qualify for mortgage ever.
3. **Credit score below 500 with no improvement plan** — too far from any loan threshold to recover in 36 months
4. **Criminal background that affects housing** — sex offender registry, violent felonies (varies by jurisdiction)
5. **Eviction history within last 2 years** — red flag for reliability
6. **Cannot afford option fee** — if they can't put 3-5% down, they lack the financial capacity for homeownership
7. **Unrealistic timeline expectations** — wants to buy in 3 months with a 520 credit score. Math doesn't work.
8. **DTI above 50%** — debt load too high to qualify for any mortgage program even after credit repair

## Scoring Logic

### Seller Lead Scoring Rubric

| Factor | Weight | Strong (8-10) | Moderate (5-7) | Weak (2-4) | Reject (0-1) |
|--------|--------|---------------|----------------|------------|---------------|
| **Equity position** | 25 | ≥ 30% equity | 15-29% equity | 10-14% equity | < 10% or underwater |
| **Motivation level** | 25 | Active life change + timeline pressure | Life change, no urgency | Curious, exploring options | No real motivation |
| **Payment status** | 15 | Current, never late | Current, 1 late in past year | Current but tight | 2+ months behind |
| **Property condition** | 15 | Move-in ready | Minor cosmetic needs | Needs moderate work | Major repairs needed |
| **Timeline flexibility** | 10 | Happy with 24-36 months | OK with 18-24 months | Wants 12 months max | Needs cash now |
| **Rental market** | 10 | Strong demand, rent covers PITI+ | Moderate demand, rent covers mortgage | Weak demand | No rental market |

**Scoring:**
- **Strong (80-100):** Contact immediately. High probability of closing.
- **Moderate (50-79):** Worth pursuing. One or two factors need work.
- **Weak (30-49):** Low priority. Multiple factors are marginal. May improve over time.
- **Not a fit (< 30):** Do not pursue as lease option. May fit another strategy.

### Worked Example

**Seller:** Jane, relocating from Phoenix to Denver for new job. Owns a 3BR/2BA in Chandler, AZ. Purchased at $280K, owes $180K, current value $350K. Current on payments. Property in good condition. Area has strong rental demand.

| Factor | Value | Score |
|--------|-------|-------|
| Equity | ($350K - $180K) / $350K = 48.6% | 10 |
| Motivation | Job relocation with timeline (starts in 60 days) | 9 |
| Payment status | Current, never late | 10 |
| Property condition | Move-in ready, updated kitchen | 9 |
| Timeline flexibility | Wants property handled before move, OK with 24 months | 8 |
| Rental market | Chandler, AZ — strong rental demand, rent covers PITI | 9 |

**Weighted score:** (10×25 + 9×25 + 10×15 + 9×15 + 8×10 + 9×10) / 100 = **9.3 → 93/100 = STRONG**

## Deal Economics

### Typical Numbers (Wholesale Lease Option / Cooperative Assignment)

| Component | Typical Range | Example ($250K property) |
|-----------|--------------|-------------------------|
| **Option fee from tenant-buyer** | 3-5% of purchase price | $7,500 - $12,500 |
| **Assignment fee (investor profit)** | $2,500 - $10,000 (or full option fee) | $5,000 - $10,000 |
| **Monthly rent premium** | $200-400/month above market rent | $300/month (goes to seller, not investor in wholesale) |
| **Purchase price** | At or slightly above current market value | $255,000 - $265,000 |
| **Option period** | 18-36 months | 24 months |

### Cooperative Assignment Structure

In a wholesale/cooperative lease option, the investor's profit is the **assignment fee only**. The investor does NOT keep the monthly spread or the back-end purchase spread — those go to the seller.

- Investor negotiates option with seller at market value
- Investor finds tenant-buyer willing to pay 3-5% option fee
- Investor assigns option to tenant-buyer
- Investor keeps a portion or all of the option fee as assignment fee
- Seller and tenant-buyer deal directly from that point

**Investor's only cost:** time to find and match seller + buyer. No financing, no closing costs, no property management.

### Worked Example

| Step | Detail | Amount |
|------|--------|--------|
| Property value | 3BR/2BA in Mesa, AZ | $250,000 |
| Investor locks option with seller | Purchase price: $250,000, option period: 24 months | $0 cost to investor |
| Investor finds tenant-buyer | Buyer pays 5% option fee | $12,500 |
| Investor assigns option | Keeps $7,500, passes $5,000 to seller as consideration | **$7,500 profit** |
| Ongoing | Seller and tenant-buyer deal directly. Investor is done. | — |

## Common Objections

### Seller Objections (Top 7)

| # | Objection | Response |
|---|-----------|----------|
| 1 | **"What if someone tears up my house?"** | Tenant-buyers are fundamentally different from renters. They put $5K-$12K non-refundable option fee down — they have serious skin in the game. They undergo background checks, have stable income, and their goal is to BUY the home, not just rent it. |
| 2 | **"When will I get cashed out?"** | Typical option period is 18-24 months. During that time, your mortgage is covered by the tenant-buyer's rent. You get your full price at closing when they exercise the option. |
| 3 | **"Why not just list with an agent?"** | You save 6-7% in commissions ($15K-$25K on a $300K home). No showings, no open houses, no months on market. We find you a qualified buyer who covers your payments from day one. |
| 4 | **"What about maintenance costs?"** | We negotiate a repair threshold in the contract. Tenant-buyer handles repairs under $500 (since they're treating it as their home). Major items above the threshold are discussed case by case. |
| 5 | **"What if the tenant-buyer doesn't buy?"** | You keep the non-refundable option fee ($5K-$12K), you've had your mortgage paid for 18-24 months, and the property has likely appreciated. You can re-lease-option to a new buyer or sell traditionally. |
| 6 | **"I've never heard of this. Is it legal?"** | Lease options have been used in real estate for decades. They're standard contracts recognized in all 50 states. We use attorney-reviewed documents. It's simply a lease combined with an option to purchase — two well-established legal instruments. |
| 7 | **"I need the money now for my next house."** | If you need full equity immediately, a lease option isn't the right fit. But if you can wait 18-24 months while someone covers your payments, you'll likely get a better price with no agent commissions. |

### Tenant-Buyer Objections (Top 7)

| # | Objection | Response |
|---|-----------|----------|
| 1 | **"What's the option fee? Why do I have to pay that?"** | The option fee secures your right to buy the home at today's price. It's typically 3-5% and applies toward your down payment when you exercise the option. Think of it as locking in your future home while you get mortgage-ready. |
| 2 | **"What if I can't buy at the end of the term?"** | You can work to extend the option period. However, the option fee is non-refundable — it's the cost of holding the property off the market for you. That's why we screen carefully upfront to make sure you have a realistic path to mortgage approval. |
| 3 | **"Is this a scam? Rent-to-own sounds too good to be true."** | This is a standard real estate transaction with attorney-reviewed contracts. You'll have a lease agreement and a separate option agreement, both legally binding. We recommend you have your own attorney review the documents before signing. |
| 4 | **"Why can't I just rent and save up?"** | You can. But rents increase every year, home prices increase, and you don't lock in a purchase price. A lease option lets you live in the home now, lock today's price, and build toward ownership while your rent credits accumulate. |
| 5 | **"What happens to my money if I walk away?"** | The option fee is non-refundable. Monthly rent credits toward the purchase are also forfeited if you don't exercise. This is why we work with you to make sure you can realistically qualify before signing. |
| 6 | **"Who pays for repairs?"** | Typically the tenant-buyer handles minor repairs (under $500) since you're treating this as your future home. Major items are the seller's responsibility per the contract. This is negotiated upfront and documented in the lease. |
| 7 | **"Can I make changes to the home?"** | Minor cosmetic changes (paint, landscaping) are usually fine. Structural changes need seller approval. Once you exercise the option and own the home, it's fully yours. |

## Legal Considerations (2025-2026)

- **Pennsylvania:** Wholesale Real Estate Transaction Transparency and Protection Act (Jan 2025) — requires specific contract disclosures for wholesale deals
- **Connecticut:** HB 7287 (effective July 2026) — mandates registration with Department of Consumer Protection for wholesaling
- **General:** Always use attorney-reviewed contracts. Lease option agreements vary by state. Some states treat them as equitable interest, others don't.
- **Fair Housing:** All tenant-buyer screening must comply with the Fair Housing Act — consistent criteria applied equally to all applicants
